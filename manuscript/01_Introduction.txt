1. Introduction

Single-shot diffraction with X-ray free-electron lasers provides a way to observe individual particles, droplets, nanoparticles, viruses, and macromolecular complexes under ultrashort-pulse conditions. In many experiments, the measured detector image contains low-q information about particle size, projected shape, approximate orientation, and coarse morphology, even when it is insufficient for unique three-dimensional reconstruction. Fast forward simulation is therefore useful for morphology screening, orientation-library generation, synthetic-data construction, and validation of analysis workflows.

The interpretation of a single low-q diffraction image is intrinsically ambiguous. Detector gaps, beamstop masking, photon-counting noise, fluence variation, background scattering, and unknown orientation all reduce the information available from one shot, and different three-dimensional objects can produce similar two-dimensional low-q projections. A practical goal is therefore not to infer a unique structure from one image, but to test whether a physically plausible candidate model can reproduce the dominant measured features. This requires a forward model that can be evaluated repeatedly over candidate structures, sizes, density contrasts, and orientations.

Several forward-modeling strategies are available. Analytical form factors are efficient for simple objects such as spheres, ellipsoids, cylinders, and shells, but they do not naturally cover arbitrary shapes. Atomistic or bead-based calculations can represent detailed molecular structures, but repeated evaluation over large orientation libraries can be costly. Dense voxel grids and three-dimensional FFT approaches are flexible, but they often allocate a full reciprocal-space volume when only a selected low-q detector-plane region is needed. Full source-to-detector simulation frameworks remain essential when pulse propagation, sample damage, detector response, photon statistics, and background are part of the question, but they can be heavier than necessary for rapid sample-level pattern generation.

This work addresses the intermediate problem of fast, geometry-general, sample-level forward simulation. The aim is to convert a candidate particle morphology and orientation into an ideal low-q detector-plane intensity pattern, while leaving beamline-specific detector response, fluence normalization, photon statistics, and background modeling to subsequent workflow layers. The method is intended as a compact particle-scattering kernel that can be embedded in larger analyses, not as a replacement for complete experimental simulation.

We use oriented surface meshes as the central representation. A closed, consistently oriented triangular mesh defines the boundary of a uniform or piecewise-uniform density domain. In the low-q projection regime, the projected electron-density contrast is proportional to the path length through the object along the incident-beam direction. Instead of filling the particle with voxels or point scatterers, the method computes this projected density by signed surface-depth rasterization, where each projected mesh face contributes a depth term with a sign determined by the orientation of its outward normal relative to the beam direction. The projected-density image is then transformed to detector-plane scattering amplitudes using a chirp z-transform. The workflow is summarized in Figure 1.

This representation covers a broad morphology domain. Analytical reference shapes can be meshed for validation, polyhedral and multiple-object models can be simulated without deriving new form factors, scalar-field objects can be converted to triangular surfaces by isosurface extraction, and composite objects can be represented as linear combinations of mesh-defined density domains. Protein-scale density models can also be approximated by constructing smooth electron-density fields from atomic coordinates and extracting isosurfaces at selected density levels.

An important requirement of the present study is reproducibility without private experimental data. Earlier versions of this project were motivated partly by experimental droplet images, including coned or deformed droplets, but those data are not used as public validation data here. Instead, the validation strategy is organized around analytical references, synthetic shape tests, performance benchmarks, and public CXIDB datasets. This makes the numerical and application-level claims reproducible by other researchers and suitable for distribution through a public code repository.

The validation is organized in three levels. First, numerical tests compare projected densities and scattering intensities with analytical or high-precision references for spheres, ellipsoids, boxes, polyhedral objects, and scalar-field models. These tests check projected-density accuracy, projected-mass conservation, mesh-resolution convergence, detector-plane sampling, and chirp-z-transform scaling. Second, benchmarks evaluate runtime and memory scaling with detector-plane image size, mesh complexity, orientation-library generation, and mesh construction. Third, public-data validation uses CXIDB nanoparticle data to compare a symmetry-aware template library against a radial-profile-preserving control.

As a further extension, we examine whether the same workflow can represent protein-scale electron density. A public GroEL structure is converted into a smooth density approximation, isosurfaces are extracted, and model assemblies are generated using mesh replication and symmetry-aware orientation sampling. This part of the work is not presented as high-confidence orientation retrieval from single GroEL shots; rather, it tests whether protein-density-derived meshes can be handled by the same projected-density and template-generation pipeline.

The main contributions are a signed projected-density rasterization method for oriented surface meshes, its coupling to chirp-z-transform detector-plane sampling, numerical and public-data validation, extension from compact particles to protein-density-derived meshes, and organization of scripts and reduced outputs for reproducible distribution. The remainder of the manuscript describes the computational framework, validation, benchmarks, public-data applications, limitations, and conclusions.

4. Performance benchmarks and memory scaling

Section 3 established that the projected-density and CZT components reproduce analytical and synthetic references under controlled conditions. This section asks whether the same validated kernel is practical for repeated low-q detector-plane pattern generation. The benchmarks focus on detector-plane image size, q-space output size, mesh complexity, orientation-library generation, mesh construction, and memory cost relative to dense voxel representations.

The goal is not to report a universal hardware-independent speed. Absolute timings depend on the GPU, CPU-GPU transfer behavior, Python overhead, implementation details, and memory management. The results are used instead to identify dominant cost components and to show why the projected-density representation is useful when only a low-q detector-plane region is required.

4.1. Benchmark scope and environment

The benchmarks used public-safe analytical or synthetic morphologies: a sphere, an ellipsoid, a two-droplet composite object, an icosahedron, and a Gaussian-blob scalar-field object. Private deformation classes, experimental detector frames, detector masks, and beamline geometry files were excluded.

The full benchmark used the signed_raster_gpu projection backend on a Tesla T4 GPU. Each timing condition used two warm-up evaluations followed by five measured repeats. Unless otherwise stated, representative single-pattern benchmarks used a 512 by 512 projected-density image and a 257 by 257 detector-plane q-grid. The odd q-grid retained the central q=0 sample, allowing the benchmark outputs to preserve a direct projected-mass or volume-conservation check. Additional software, hardware, and timing-scope details are given in Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Note 4.

4.2. Single-pattern projection and CZT timing

Single-pattern timing separated signed projected-density rasterization from the CZT detector-plane transform. For the sphere model, increasing the projected-density image from 256 by 256 to 512 by 512 and 1024 by 1024 pixels changed the median total time from 12.66 ms to 26.01 ms and 87.89 ms. At the standard 512 by 512 image and 257 by 257 q-grid setting, the benchmark morphologies produced median total times of approximately 24 to 34 ms.

The increase with image size reflected both rasterization and transform work. For the sphere, the CZT component grew from 7.15 ms at 256 by 256 pixels to 57.16 ms at 1024 by 1024 pixels, making detector-plane transform sampling a major cost component at larger image sizes. Detailed per-size and per-morphology timing values are listed in Supplementary Note 4.

4.3. q-grid scaling

The q-grid benchmark fixed the projected-density image size at 512 by 512 pixels and varied the reciprocal-space output grid. Increasing the q-grid from 129 by 129 to 257 by 257 and 513 by 513 increased median total time for the sphere from 23.28 ms to 25.27 ms and 37.47 ms. The Gaussian-blob model showed the same trend, increasing from 21.65 ms to 25.42 ms and 37.74 ms.

Thus, q-space output size primarily affects the transform stage. For template-library construction, the q-grid should match the detector region and comparison metric needed for the scientific question; oversampling beyond the useful low-q region directly increases transform cost.

4.4. Mesh-complexity and morphology dependence

Mesh-complexity scaling showed that face count alone did not determine runtime. At the standard detector and q-grid setting, sphere meshes from 224 to 36,480 faces all produced median total times near 24 ms, while the 65,024-face mesh increased to 34.56 ms. The morphology benchmark gave the same interpretation: the two-droplet, sphere, ellipsoid, and Gaussian-blob models were similar in timing, whereas the 20-face icosahedron was slower because its projected-fragment workload was less favorable.

These results indicate that total runtime depends on mesh size, projected pixel coverage, rasterized triangle fragments, detector image size, and CZT output size. Projected workload is therefore more informative than topology size alone.

4.5. Orientation-library throughput

Template-library generation repeats projection and transform evaluations over many orientations. For 1,000-orientation libraries at the standard detector and q-grid setting, all five benchmark morphologies completed without failed orientations. The total wall-clock times were approximately 151 to 160 s, corresponding to end-to-end throughputs of about 6.25 to 6.61 patterns per second.

This wall-clock throughput is lower than the single-pattern kernel timing alone would suggest. For the 1,000-orientation sphere benchmark, summed projection and CZT times accounted for 26.92 s of the measured 154.54 s wall-clock time. The remaining cost reflects Python-loop overhead, per-orientation setup, result handling, GPU synchronization, and other implementation-level overhead. The public script therefore demonstrates feasible library generation while identifying batching, reduced synchronization, and optimized orchestration as improvement targets.

4.6. Mesh-generation cost

Mesh generation was benchmarked separately because it is usually a per-model rather than per-orientation cost. Direct analytical and component-based meshes required only a few milliseconds. Scalar-field-derived Gaussian-blob meshes were more expensive but still practical at the tested sizes, with field generation and VTK Flying Edges extraction remaining below 80 ms for the largest 160 by 160 by 160 grid.

This separation is useful for large library workflows. A candidate morphology can be generated, repaired, and stored once, after which projection and CZT are repeated over the orientation library. For scalar-field or protein-density-derived models, one-time mesh construction should therefore be reported separately from repeated pattern generation.

4.7. Memory-cost comparison with dense voxel approaches

The main structural advantage of the projected-density workflow is memory scaling. A dense three-dimensional voxel representation scales as O(N^3), whereas the projected-density image scales as O(N^2). When the output of interest is a selected detector-plane low-q region, the projected-density workflow avoids allocating unused three-dimensional reciprocal-space volume.

For a 512 grid, a dense float32 voxel volume requires 512 MB and a dense complex64 volume requires 1,024 MB. In contrast, a 512 by 512 projected float32 image requires 1 MB and a projected complex64 image requires 2 MB. Mesh arrays add memory cost, but the tested mesh-array estimates remained in the megabyte range rather than the gigabyte range. Additional memory values are given in Supplementary Note 4.

This comparison does not mean that the projected-density method replaces full three-dimensional diffraction simulation. Dense voxel and three-dimensional FFT approaches remain appropriate when volumetric reciprocal-space sampling, high-q structure, or a more complete wave-optical model is required. The advantage shown here is specific to repeated generation of ideal low-q detector-plane patterns from candidate morphologies.

4.8. Performance summary

The benchmarks show that the validated projected-density kernel is practical for repeated low-q pattern generation on the tested GPU system. Representative single-pattern generation at 512 by 512 pixels and a 257 by 257 q-grid required approximately 24 to 34 ms across the benchmark morphologies. Detector image size and q-grid size increased runtime as expected, with CZT becoming a major cost component at larger output grids.

End-to-end 1,000-orientation library generation was feasible, but the current public script showed substantial wall-clock overhead beyond the summed projection and CZT times. This is an implementation-level optimization opportunity rather than a limitation of the numerical kernel itself. The clearest structural result is memory scaling: when only a detector-plane low-q region is required, the workflow avoids the O(N^3) cost of dense voxel sampling and instead uses O(N^2) projected images plus compact mesh arrays.

These results define the computational scope for the application-level studies in Section 5. The method is best suited to workflows that require many ideal sample-level low-q patterns for screening, template-library construction, retrieval tests, or control comparisons, while leaving detector response and experiment-specific count modeling to separate layers.

7. Conclusion

This study developed and evaluated a projected-density forward-simulation framework for low-q single-shot diffraction patterns from mesh-defined particle models. The method represents uniform or piecewise-uniform density domains with closed, consistently oriented triangular surface meshes, computes projected density by signed surface-depth rasterization, and samples detector-plane scattering intensities using chirp z-transform sampling. The result is a compact sample-level scattering kernel for morphology screening, orientation-library generation, synthetic-data construction, and public-data comparison.

The numerical validation showed that the projection and transform operations behave as intended. Analytical and synthetic tests using spheres, ellipsoids, boxes, polyhedral objects, and scalar-field objects confirmed projected-density accuracy, projected-mass conservation, mesh-resolution convergence, and q-space sampling consistency. Smooth meshed objects agreed with analytical references at approximately the 10^-3 relative-error level, while the exact polyhedral box case showed substantially smaller intensity error. The q=0 amplitude checks and orientation sweeps further confirmed conservation of mesh-defined projected mass.

The performance results support the practical role of the method. When the target is a selected low-q detector-plane region, the workflow avoids dense three-dimensional reciprocal-space allocation and instead uses a two-dimensional projected image, a two-dimensional q-grid, and compact mesh arrays. In the tested GPU environment, representative single-pattern generation was in the tens-of-milliseconds range. The orientation-library benchmarks also showed that additional throughput gains should be possible through batching, reduced synchronization, and tighter GPU orchestration.

The public-data applications define the main scientific scope of the framework. In CXIDB ID57, a 240-orientation symmetry-aware core-shell nanoparticle library recovered all noisy synthetic queries exactly and outperformed a radial-profile-preserving randomized control in all 209 public experimental paired comparisons. This supports the claim that the projected-density library captures model-specific low-q features beyond a radial intensity envelope, while still remaining a candidate-model validation rather than proof of ground-truth orientation recovery for individual shots.

The GroEL case showed that the workflow can be extended to protein-density-derived mesh models. A public PDB structure was converted into a smooth electron-density grid, isosurfaces were extracted, heptamer and tetradecamer assemblies were built, and decimated meshes preserved projection-level behavior. The resulting D7 symmetry-aware template library gave a small but consistent improvement over a radial control for curated public shots. This result is best interpreted as a modeling and scalability demonstration, not as high-confidence molecular orientation retrieval.

Overall, the framework fills an intermediate role between simple analytical form factors and full source-to-detector experimental simulations. It is geometry-general, reproducible with public data, efficient for low-q detector-plane sampling, and extensible from compact particles to protein-density-derived meshes. Its appropriate use is to generate and test physically motivated candidate patterns under explicit sample-level assumptions, while detector response, photon statistics, background modeling, and full experimental calibration remain separate workflow layers.
